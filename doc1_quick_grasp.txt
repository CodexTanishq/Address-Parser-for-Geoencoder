PS-3 LOCATION PREDICTION
Quick-Grasp Report & Senior Presentation Guide
Predict where an address actually is — and quantify how confident we are
30-second version
We have messy address text, a commercial geocoder, and noisy field-visit GPS data. Some visits are genuine, some may be unreliable, and many addresses have no visit at all. The proposed system cleans the data, estimates how trustworthy each visit is, builds an initial location prior, combines evidence using Bayesian fusion, and finally outputs a predicted location plus a calibrated confidence radius.
Question
Answer
What is the task?
Predict the actual location of an address and provide a confidence region around the prediction.
What is the baseline?
A commercial geocoder already produces a location from address text; its error is measured against surveyed truth.
What makes the problem hard?
Noisy GPS visits, potentially fake visits, messy Indian address text, duplicate/related addresses, and ~55% addresses with no useful visit evidence.
What is the core idea?
Treat every clue as evidence with different reliability rather than treating every GPS point equally.
Source: PS-3 Research Guide — Pages 1, 2, 11, 17 and 23.

3. Ground Truth, Baseline & Error
Ground truth
`surveyed_addresses.csv` contains the surveyed true location. This is what we compare our prediction against during evaluation.
Baseline geocoder
The guide reports a commercial geocoder baseline. It takes address text and returns a location plus a precision label such as rooftop, street, locality, or pincode. This is not the field-agent GPS.
Geocoder precision
Median error in guide
Interpretation
rooftop
26 m
Usually very precise
street
109 m
Reasonably precise
locality
386 m
Much broader location
pincode
1,376 m
Very coarse location
Reported baseline score
Across the 100 surveyed addresses, the guide reports a median geocoder error of about 376 m and a 90th-percentile error of about 839 m. This is the benchmark we try to improve on.
How one error is calculated
For each labelled address, compare the predicted point with the surveyed true point. In the local (x, y) grid used by the guide:
error = sqrt((x_pred - x_true)^2 + (y_pred - y_true)^2)
• Do this for every surveyed address → one error per address.
• Median error = middle of the sorted errors (for 100 cases, the 50th/51st average).
• P90 error = error value below which 90% of cases fall.
Source: PS-3 Research Guide — Pages 1, 3, 4 and 16.
4. Why We Cannot Trust Every Visit Equally
The guide analyzes how close a visit check-in is to the true house, and shows that the visit outcome matters a lot. This is the motivation for assigning different levels of trust to different visits.

The key idea
The visit outcome is already recorded in field_visits.csv. Our model does not need to predict whether the agent met the customer; it can use the recorded outcome as an input feature when estimating how much to trust that visit.
Multiple visits
Repeated independent visits are stronger evidence because agreement between visits helps distinguish a real cluster from an isolated outlier. The guide reports median errors of roughly 40 m (1 visit), 14 m (2), 8 m (3) and 23 m (4+).
Important nuance
The point is not that “more visits always means lower median error” in a perfectly monotonic way. The key insight is that with only one visit we cannot tell whether it is an outlier; multiple independent visits provide consistency information.
Source: PS-3 Research Guide — Pages 4-5.

7. Bayesian Fusion — The Core Mathematical Idea
The core formula
P(L | evidence) ∝ P(L) × ∏ P(V_i | L)
Symbol
Meaning
L
A candidate true location
P(L)
Prior: our initial belief about that location
V_i
The i-th visit / piece of visit evidence
P(V_i | L)
Likelihood: how compatible that visit is with location L
P(L | evidence)
Posterior: our updated belief after combining the evidence
Why this formula?
The expression comes from Bayes’ theorem. The product form assumes the visits are conditionally independent given the true location. The theorem itself is exact; whether this particular modeling assumption works best must be tested empirically.
A simple mental model
• Prior: “From the address text + geocoder, I think the house is probably around Area A.”
• Likelihood: “If the house were in Area A, how plausible is this visit GPS?”
• Posterior: “After seeing all visits and clues, where do I believe the house is most likely to be?”
Where do the probabilities come from?
The prior is built from initial location evidence such as text, landmarks, geocoder and locality. Visit likelihood can be modeled from GPS distance/uncertainty plus a trust probability for whether the visit is genuine.
Source: PS-3 Research Guide — Pages 11-12.
8. How the Visit Probability Is Modeled
For the core probabilistic model, the guide writes each observed GPS point as a mixture of a genuine visit component and an outlier component:
p(z_i | μ) = π_i × N(z_i; μ, σ_i² I) + (1 − π_i) × Outlier(z_i)
Term
Plain-English meaning
z_i
Observed GPS position of visit i
μ
Candidate true house location
σ_i
GPS uncertainty for visit i; related to GPS accuracy
π_i
Probability the visit is genuine / trustworthy
N(...)
Gaussian “bell curve” around the candidate house location
Outlier(...)
A broad model for a visit that may not come from the house
The important intuition
If a visit is close to the candidate house and has good GPS accuracy, the genuine-visit likelihood is high. If the visit is far away or looks suspicious, the outlier component gets more weight.
Where does π_i come from?
The guide suggests predicting π_i with features such as outcome_type, GPS accuracy and fraud-pattern features, using a small classifier such as logistic regression or gradient boosting. In other words, π_i is the mathematical form of the “trust score” we discussed earlier.
Why this is useful
• Good visit → high π_i → stronger location evidence.
• Suspicious visit → low π_i → weaker location evidence.
• Bad GPS accuracy → larger uncertainty σ_i → less sharply concentrated evidence.
Source: PS-3 Research Guide — Pages 8, 12 and 17.
9. From One Point to a Probability Cloud
The guide says the beginner instinct is to predict one dot. A better approach is to maintain a probability distribution over possible locations: dense where the house is likely to be, sparse where it is unlikely.
Level
Method in guide
What it gives us
1. Robust estimation
Median, geometric median, inverse-variance weighting, DBSCAN/HDBSCAN, RANSAC
Simple robust location estimate; useful baseline methods
2. Probabilistic model
Mixture model + EM
Posterior belief while accounting for outliers
3. Grid / particle posterior
Grid-based Bayes filter / particle filter
A full spatial probability cloud; naturally handles multiple candidate locations
4. Learned models
LightGBM/XGBoost/CatBoost, residual regression, GPR, quantile regression, NGBoost, NLP models
Learn corrections, trust, error or full distributions from data
Practical reading of the levels
These are not four things that must all be stacked blindly. The guide presents them as a progression from simple to advanced. A sensible project should establish a strong simple baseline first, then add complexity only if validation shows a gain.
Address-text sub-pipeline
Normalize abbreviations (e.g. nr→near, opp→opposite, bhd→behind, Rd→road, Blk→block), transliterate where needed, extract house/street/locality/landmark entities, resolve landmarks against locality data, and combine the resulting clue with the geocoder/locality prior.

10. Confidence Radius: Proving We Are Honest
Definition
For a target coverage α (e.g. 0.80), choose radius r_α such that P(||true − pin|| ≤ r_α) = α.
In plain English: if we publish an “80% confidence circle”, then across many similar addresses, about 80% of the true locations should actually lie inside those circles.
Method
Idea
Role
Gaussian posterior
Derive radius analytically from a 2-D Gaussian under the model assumptions
Fast theoretical radius
Grid/particle posterior
Take the smallest circle containing α of the probability mass
Directly matches the probability cloud
Quantile regression
Predict the α-quantile of location error from features
Learn a radius from data
Conformal prediction
Calibrate radius using held-out errors
Recommended final calibration layer in the guide
Coverage vs sharpness
Coverage asks: “Do my circles actually contain the truth often enough?” Sharpness asks: “How small are those circles?” A huge radius can get good coverage but is not useful. The goal is calibrated AND as small as possible.
Source: PS-3 Research Guide — Pages 15-16 and 18.

13. What to Say to Seniors — Quick Answers
Q: Why not just use the geocoder?
A: Because the baseline has large errors for coarse precision levels and some addresses have no useful geocoder/visit evidence. The project is about improving beyond that baseline.
Q: Why not average every GPS visit?
A: Because visits can be noisy or suspicious. Reliability differs by visit outcome, GPS accuracy and agent/trail behaviour.
Q: Is outcome_type predicted by our model?
A: No. It is already recorded in field_visits.csv and can be used as an input feature for visit trust.
Q: What is the trust/fraud score?
A: A learned estimate of how genuine/reliable a visit appears; higher trust means stronger influence on location estimation.
Q: Why Bayesian fusion?
A: Bayes gives a principled way to combine prior location information with multiple evidence sources. We still need empirical validation to show it is effective on this dataset.
Q: Where do the P values come from?
A: The prior comes from initial location evidence; visit likelihood comes from how compatible a GPS observation is with a candidate location, considering GPS uncertainty and visit trust.
Q: Why a confidence radius?
A: A single point hides uncertainty. The project requires a calibrated region around the prediction so the system can say how uncertain it is.
Q: How do we know confidence is honest?
A: Check empirical coverage on held-out data and calibration curves; ideally an 80% region contains the truth about 80% of the time.
Q: What if there are no visits?
A: Use address text, landmarks, geocoder, locality and nearby confirmed-address information; the guide specifically proposes a cold-start evaluation.
Q: Do we need deep learning?
A: Not by default. The guide lists deep NLP as an advanced option. Start with robust statistics + engineered features + tabular ML and only add complexity if validation supports it.

Do not think: “GPS point = truth.” Think: “Each source is evidence with a reliability level. The model combines evidence and keeps uncertainty instead of pretending it knows one exact point.”
